\begin{abstract}
Words do not preserve fixed meanings across time. Without representations that 
reflect this, \texttt{token@time} queries become impossible: there is no way to ask where 
the meaning of \emph{gay} stood in 1920, or how it moved by 1980, if both 
queries return the same vector. Yet time-conditioned transformers have
rarely been tested on whether they provide an explicit vector for a word at a
specified period whose neighbors reflect how the word is used then. Knowing
that a representation encodes the period label is not the same as being able to
query such neighborhoods and compare how they change over time, a distinction
we formalize as \emph{temporal traceability}. We introduce a controlled test suite
with predefined trajectories (stable, drifting, bifurcating) to measure it directly.

Three findings emerge. Giving the model period information more than doubles
the movement of a word's neighbors relative to the non-temporal baseline.
However, adding the same period vector to every token and combining each token
with that vector produce equivalent average movement when nearby verbs and
objects are reliable cues; a difference appears when those cues are degraded.
Finally, averaged memory does not improve for bifurcating subjects, which
approximate coexisting senses. Label-derived summaries help, but two
unsupervised cluster centers do not; averaging alone therefore does not explain
the limitation.

\keywords{Temporal semantics \and Semantic change \and Transformer models \and Traceability \and Diagnostic evaluation}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Words do not preserve fixed meanings across time. In 1900, \emph{broadcast}
referred to scattering seeds across a field; today it describes a social media
post reaching millions. Lexical semantic change detection (LSCD) usually learns
vector representations from word co-occurrence in a separate corpus for each
period and then compares them~\cite{schlechtweg2020semeval,tahmasebi2021survey}.
This approach has produced important methods and benchmarks, but it summarizes
change as one similarity score between separately trained models:
\texttt{broadcast@1900} and \texttt{broadcast@2020} come from different models
and become comparable only after their spaces are aligned, rather than being
explicit queries to one model.

We call a representation \emph{temporally traceable} if it provides an explicit
vector for a word at a requested period in a common space and changes in its
nearest neighbors across periods reflect changes in how the word is used.
Consider \emph{gay}, which in the early twentieth century was mainly associated
with \emph{cheerful} and \emph{merry}, and by the late twentieth century with
\emph{queer} and \emph{lesbian}~\cite{hamilton2016diachronic}. Comparable
representations can support socially relevant questions, including whether a
word moved toward or away from stigmatized terms. Answering such questions
requires directly comparable representations for \texttt{gay@1920} and
\texttt{gay@1980}.

In this study, we test temporal traceability in a controlled environment where
the semantic path is predefined. In natural corpora, an apparent change may instead
reflect differences in word frequency, genre, or corpus composition. It is
therefore difficult to tell whether a change in the learned vectors comes from
the model architecture or the data. We introduce a corpus in which words follow
predefined stable, drifting, or bifurcating trajectories. Four diagnostics test
whether their neighbors follow these paths, rather than merely whether a
classifier can recover a context label from their representations.

Against this suite we evaluate one non-temporal baseline (Standard) and three
time-aware architectures. Additive gives every token the same period vector,
Token-Time combines each token with that vector, and Memory-Augmented also
summarizes earlier periods. We ask whether these designs improve temporal
traceability over the non-temporal baseline and what each design contributes.
Systematic evaluation on noisier regimes and natural corpora is left for future work.

The paper makes four contributions. (1) We define temporal traceability as the
ability to query an explicit word-at-period vector and test whether changes in
its neighborhood reflect changes in the word's use, rather than merely recover
a label from its representation. (2) We introduce a
controlled corpus with stable, drifting, and bifurcating trajectories and four
diagnostics that separate neighbor movement from label prediction. (3) We show
that all three time-aware architectures more than double the movement of
drifting words relative to the non-temporal baseline. Additive and Token-Time
produce equivalent average movement when contextual cues are reliable, while
Token-Time performs better on the ambiguous classifier when they are not. (4)
For bifurcating subjects, averaged memory does not improve over Token-Time.
Label-derived summaries help, but two unsupervised cluster centers do not, so
averaging alone does not explain the limitation.

\section{Related Work}
% Target length: under 1 page.
Lexical semantic change detection (LSCD) is the dominant computational framing
for meaning shift. Diachronic word embeddings~\cite{hamilton2016diachronic}
learn one vector space per time slice, align the separately trained spaces, and
measure how a word's neighbors change. We share the view that meaning change
should alter these neighbors, but use a different architecture: LSCD produces a
score comparing two spaces rather than one model that can represent
\texttt{token@time} directly. Standard benchmarks formalize this
task~\cite{schlechtweg2020semeval,tahmasebi2021survey}, but changes in frequency
and genre can also affect their scores; Spanish shared tasks show that manual
annotation is required even for modern corpora~\cite{zamora2022lscdiscovery}.
Our controlled corpus makes each semantic path known, allowing differences to
be attributed to model design rather than corpus composition.

Temporal neural language models avoid separate spaces by conditioning one model
on time. Prior work uses timestamps for knowledge-intensive QA, masked language
modeling, or period-stratified downstream
evaluation~\cite{dhingra2022timeaware,rosin2022timemasking,loureiro2022timelms}.
These results show that providing time can improve predictions for the requested
period, but do not test whether a word can be queried at different periods and
its neighborhoods inspected and compared directly.

Finally, semantic change may involve bifurcation rather than monotonic drift.
Giulianelli et al.\ show that words can develop coexisting
senses~\cite{giulianelli2020analysing}; a mean-pooled summary can blur their
distinction by falling between them. Our stable, drifting, and bifurcating
trajectories test whether one averaged vector per subject and period is
adequate, and the memory ablations examine alternatives.

\section{Controlled Diagnostic Suite}
% Target length: 1.0 page.
We construct a corpus of subject--verb--object (SVO) sentences spanning ten
periods. Every sentence consists of one subject, one verb, and one object. We
divide the 8 verbs and 8 objects
into two groups, $N_1$ and $N_2$, which act as distinct semantic contexts:
$N_1$ uses
\(\{V1,\ldots,V4\}\) and \(\{O1,\ldots,O4\}\); $N_2$ uses
\(\{V5,\ldots,V8\}\) and \(\{O5,\ldots,O8\}\). A sentence generated from
$N_1$ therefore looks like \texttt{S5 V2 O3}, while one from $N_2$
looks like \texttt{S5 V6 O8}; the subject token is the same in both cases, but
its co-occurring words differ. For each of the 30 subjects,
\(P(N_1 \mid s, t)\) is the probability that a sentence containing subject
\(s\) at period \(t\) is drawn from $N_1$; its evolution from \(t0\) through
\(t9\) defines the subject's predefined trajectory.

\caption{Controlled diagnostic suite overview. \emph{Left}: SVO sentences for a drifting

at \(t7\) (marked \({\ast}\)) illustrates the deliberate noise in contextual cues.
\emph{Center}: predefined \(P(N_1 \mid s, t)\) trajectories for Stable (flat),

\label{fig:benchmark-design}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-design} (center) shows the three predefined trajectory
classes. Stable subjects maintain an approximately constant \(P(N_1 \mid s, t)\)
throughout. Drift subjects follow a monotonic trajectory from near-exclusive
$N_1$ (\(P(N_1 \mid s, t) \approx 1\) at \(t0\)) to near-exclusive $N_2$
(\(P(N_1 \mid s, t) \approx 0\) at \(t9\)): a drifting subject that appears in
sentences like \texttt{S5 V2 O3} early on will appear in sentences like
\texttt{S5 V6 O8} by the final period. Bifurcating subjects begin in $N_1$
and move toward a mixed state (\(P(N_1 \mid s, t) \approx 0.5\)) where both
neighborhoods appear with equal frequency, approximating two coexisting senses. The
suite contains 10 subjects per class.

Each verb and object is drawn from the opposite context group 25\% of the time
(Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-design}, left), so the model cannot recover the
intended context from the accompanying words alone; it must use subject
identity together with the period. The \texttt{true\_context} label is withheld
from encoder training and used only by the diagnostics.

This design separates differences caused by the architecture from differences
in frequency, genre, or corpus composition. Because \(P(N_1 \mid s,t)\) is
known, we can test whether a model's nearest neighbors follow the process that
generated the corpus. The scope of these controlled diagnostics is revisited in
the Discussion.

The experiments use four data conditions. The standard and ambiguous
conditions vary the reliability of the accompanying words. In the standard
condition, after $N_1$ or $N_2$ is selected to generate a sentence, each verb
and object is drawn from that same group with 75\% probability, matching the
distribution used for masked language modeling (MLM) training. In the
ambiguous condition, this probability is reduced to 50\%, so the verb and
object do not reveal which group generated the sentence on average. The
contrastive condition presents the same masked-verb subject--object frame with
two period labels and measures whether changing only the period changes the
preferred context. The continuation condition contains periods \(t8\)--\(t9\),
which are excluded from MLM training on \(t0\)--\(t7\).

\section{Time-Aware Architectures}
% Target length: 2.0 pages.
We evaluate one non-temporal baseline and three time-aware architectures,
all sharing the same Transformer encoder backbone. Standard (no period input) is
the non-temporal baseline. The three time-aware architectures differ in how
they introduce time: Additive gives every token the same period vector;
Token-Time combines each token with that vector before the encoder; and
Memory-Augmented also lets the subject consult summaries of earlier periods.

All models follow the same pipeline based on the Transformer
encoder~\cite{vaswani2017attention}. A sentence is tokenized as
\texttt{[CLS] S V O [SEP]}. Each token \(w_i\) is mapped to an input embedding
\(z_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\). The sequence \((z_1,\ldots,z_n)\) is processed by a
Transformer encoder, and the final hidden state at the subject position,
\[
  (h_1,\ldots,h_n) = \mathrm{Encoder}(z_1,\ldots,z_n),
  \qquad h_s = h_{\mathrm{subj}},
\]
is the \texttt{token@time} representation inspected by the diagnostics. All
models are trained with MLM~\cite{devlin2019bert}: some tokens are masked, and the model reconstructs
them from the remaining tokens and, when available, the period input. The
variants differ in how they inject temporal information before the encoder.

The three time-aware models use a shared period vector
\(\tau(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d\), a learned projection of sinusoidal features of
\(t/T\), where \(T=10\) is the number of corpus periods. It is distinct from the
position encoding \(p_i\), which marks subject, verb, or object position.
Additive and Token-Time differ in how they combine \(\tau(t)\) with token
identity; Memory-Augmented lets the subject embedding consult vectors from
earlier periods. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} summarizes the comparison.

\subsection{Baseline: Standard Transformer}
Standard is the null hypothesis: no period signal is available, so any temporal
behavior must arise from changes in the observed words alone.
\[
  z_i = e(w_i) + p_i .
\]

\subsection{Additive Time-Conditioned Transformer}
Additive tests the simplest use of time: adding the same period vector
\(\tau(t)\) to every token:

\[
  z_i = e(w_i) + p_i + \tau(t).
\]

\texttt{S5 V2 O3} therefore receives different inputs at \(t0\) and \(t9\):
each token is combined with \(\tau(0)\) or \(\tau(9)\). The same period vector
is added at every position, so time enters each token in the same way.

\subsection{Token-Time Transformer}
Token-Time tests a learned alternative to direct addition. It concatenates each
token embedding with \(\tau(t)\) and projects the resulting \(2d\)-dimensional
vector back to \(d\) dimensions:

\[
  z_i = W \cdot \operatorname{concat}\!\left(e(w_i), \tau(t)\right) + p_i ,
\]

where \(\operatorname{concat}\) joins the two \(d\)-dimensional vectors into a
\(2d\)-dimensional vector, and the learned projection matrix
\(W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 2d}\) maps it back to \(d\) dimensions. The
comparison with Additive tests whether this projection helps beyond adding the
period vector directly.

\subsection{Memory-Augmented Extension}
The Memory-Augmented extension tests whether representations from earlier
periods add useful information beyond the current token-time embedding. It
begins with the same input embeddings as Token-Time. For a sentence containing
\texttt{S5} at \(t7\), it builds embeddings for
\texttt{[CLS] S5 V O [SEP]} and modifies only the subject embedding before the
Transformer encoder sees the sentence.

The memory stores one past-period summary vector (often called a prototype)
\(m(s,t)\) for each subject and period. This vector is the mean of the final
hidden states \(h_s\) from MLM training sentences containing subject \(s\) at
period \(t\). When processing a sentence at period \(t\), the model can access
only summary vectors from earlier periods. At \(t7\), for example, it can use
the \texttt{S5} vectors from \(t0\) through \(t6\), but not from \(t7\) or
later.

The model uses attention to combine these past vectors. The current subject
embedding \(z_s\) is the query, while \(M_s\), with one past-period vector per
row, provides the keys and values:

\[
  r_s = \mathrm{Attn}(z_s, M_s, M_s).
\]

The resulting vector \(r_s\) is a weighted combination of the past-period
vectors. A learned scalar gate \(g\) controls how much this summary changes the
current subject embedding:

\[
  \tilde{z}_s =
  z_s + g \cdot
  \left(\mathrm{LN}(z_s + r_s) - z_s\right).
\]

Here, \(\mathrm{LN}\) denotes layer normalization. Because \(g\) is initialized
at zero, the extension initially behaves exactly like Token-Time. Finally,
\(\tilde{z}_s\) replaces \(z_s\) in the input sequence. Verb and object
embeddings are unchanged, so the subject is the only token that enters the
encoder with information from earlier periods.

\section{Evaluation Protocol and Diagnostics}
% Target length: 1.25--1.5 pages.
We evaluate \(h_s\) with four diagnostics. Across these diagnostics, we compare
each time-aware model with Standard, Token-Time with Additive, and
Memory-Augmented with Token-Time. These comparisons measure, respectively, the
effects of providing time, using a learned projection instead of direct
addition, and consulting earlier representations.

\textbf{D1: Linear classifier~\cite{conneau2018probing}.} D1 tests whether a linear
classifier can tell from \(h_s\) whether the sentence was generated from
\(N_1\) or \(N_2\). We freeze the encoder and train the classifier on representations
from the standard condition. Its standard-condition accuracy is in-sample; the
ambiguous condition tests the same classifier without retraining it. High
accuracy does not show how the representation's nearest neighbors change across
periods.

\textbf{D2: Context drift score.} For each evaluation sentence, we use \(h_s\),
the final representation of its subject, as the query. For example, for
\texttt{S12 V2 O7} at \(t0\), the query is the vector at the \texttt{S12}
position. We compare it with the subject representations from all other
evaluation sentences and retrieve the \(k=10\) nearest by cosine similarity.
The score is the proportion of neighbors whose \texttt{true\_context} is $N_1$:
if 8 of the 10 neighbors come from $N_1$, the score is 0.8. We average this
score by trajectory class and period. The reported \(\Delta\) is the score at
\(t9\) minus the score at \(t0\); for example, a decrease from 0.8 to 0.2 gives
\(\Delta=-0.6\), indicating movement from $N_1$ toward $N_2$. We also compare
the per-period scores with the predefined \(P(N_1 \mid s,t)\) trajectories.

\textbf{D3: Contrastive test.} D3 asks whether changing only the period changes
the masked-verb prediction in the correct direction. Each of the 29 pairs
contains the same subject--object frame at an early and a late period. For
example, the model receives \texttt{S12 [MASK] O2} at both \(t0\) and \(t9\).
At each period, we sum the predicted probabilities for the $N_1$ verbs
(\(V1\)--\(V4\)) and for the $N_2$ verbs (\(V5\)--\(V8\)). The pair counts as
a correct sign flip if the $N_1$ total is higher at \(t0\) and the $N_2$ total
is higher at \(t9\). A sign-flip rate of 0.3 means that 30\% of the pairs meet
both conditions.

\textbf{D4: Continuation diagnostic.} D4 trains a separate linear classifier on
standard-condition representations from \(t0\)--\(t7\) and evaluates it without
retraining on continuation sentences from \(t8\)--\(t9\). For example, for
\texttt{S12 V6 O7} at \(t8\), the classifier receives the vector at the
\texttt{S12} position and should identify $N_2$ as the group that generated the
sentence. Accuracy is the proportion of correct $N_1$/$N_2$ predictions.

\textbf{Auxiliary layer-wise comparison.} We also compute linear centered
kernel alignment (CKA) between models on the same standard held-out sentences,
at the input and after each encoder layer. CKA compares their representation
patterns; higher values indicate greater similarity~\cite{kornblith2019similarity}.

\textbf{Memory ablation.} For Memory-Augmented, we also test an oracle version
that replaces each learned
summary vector with one built from the known context labels. For example, if
80\% of a subject's training sentences at a given period come from $N_1$, its
oracle summary is \(0.8v_1+0.2v_2\), where the fixed orthogonal vectors
\(v_1\) and \(v_2\) represent $N_1$ and $N_2$. The attention and gate are
unchanged. Because the ordinary model does not receive these labels, the oracle
serves only as an analysis tool.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
All models share the same Transformer encoder: \(d=64\), 4 attention heads, 2
layers, feed-forward width 128, dropout 0.1. The period encoding projects 32
sinusoidal features of \(t/T\) to \(d\) via a two-layer MLP. Training uses
AdamW (lr \(=10^{-3}\), weight decay \(=10^{-2}\)) with a cosine annealing
schedule, 30 epochs, batch size 64. The corpus contains 5{,}000 SVO sentences;
MLM training uses 3{,}159 sentences from periods \(t0\)--\(t7\); 763 sentences
form the standard held-out set used for D1--D2 and to train the D1 classifier;
the 615 sentences from \(t0\)--\(t7\) also train the separate D4 classifier. Another 794
sentences from \(t8\)--\(t9\) are reserved for D4 and excluded from both MLM and
classifier training. Validation sentences from all ten periods are used to
select the version of each model evaluated in the diagnostics. Thus, D4 tests
whether a classifier trained on \(t0\)--\(t7\) transfers to later sentences; it
is not a fully held-out forecast because model selection uses validation data
from \(t8\)--\(t9\).

\section{Results}
% Target length: 2.5--3.0 pages.
We report means and 95\% confidence intervals over 31 training seeds.
Giving the model a period more than doubles the change in the neighbors of
drifting subjects: all three time-aware models reach
\(\Delta\approx-0.56\), compared with \(-0.221\) for Standard. Additive and
Token-Time produce nearly identical changes, and adding memory does not
improve over Token-Time.

\subsection{Changes in Nearest Neighbors}
% Main claim: temporal conditioning produces stronger neighborhood transitions than Standard.
% Use class-specific curves to avoid claiming stable subjects are perfectly flat.
Figure~\ref{fig:context-drift} and Table~\ref{tab:context-drift} show how the
proportion of \(N_1\) neighbors changes across periods. For drifting subjects,
Standard decreases by \(0.221\), with a 95\% CI of
\([-0.240,-0.203]\). Token-Time decreases by \(0.563\), more than twice as
much. Additive produces a similar decrease of \(0.567\).

Stable subjects should not move toward either context, but the time-aware
models also shift them toward \(N_2\), with \(\Delta\) between \(-0.150\) and
\(-0.180\). This unwanted movement is much smaller than the change of about
\(-0.56\) for drifting subjects, but suggests that the period signal affects
all subjects. Bifurcating subjects fall between these cases: the time-aware
models reach values near \(0.53\) at \(t9\), close to their predefined 50--50
mixture.

We used a paired equivalence test to determine whether the Additive and
Token-Time scores are sufficiently close, rather than merely failing to find a
difference. We treated absolute differences below \(0.05\) as equivalent. The
mean paired difference (Additive minus Token-Time) is \(-0.003\), with a 90\%
CI of \([-0.026,+0.019]\); the TOST supports equivalence
(\(p_\mathrm{TOST}<0.001\)). Equivalence also holds with the narrower margin
of \(0.034\) (\(p_\mathrm{TOST}=0.014\)).

\subsection{Classifier and Contrastive Diagnostics}
The linear classifier reveals a difference that the drift score does not. In
the standard condition, verbs and objects indicate the correct context 75\% of
the time. All time-aware models outperform Standard
(\(0.875\) [\(\pm\)0.005]): Additive reaches \(0.892\)
[\(\pm\)0.003], while Token-Time and Memory-Augmented both reach \(0.881\)
([\(\pm\)0.005] and [\(\pm\)0.006], respectively).

The ordering changes in the ambiguous condition, where verbs and objects
provide no reliable context clue. Standard reaches \(0.565\)
\([\pm0.004]\), Additive \(0.579\) \([\pm0.005]\), Token-Time \(0.595\)
\([\pm0.006]\), and Memory-Augmented \(0.598\) \([\pm0.008]\).
Token-Time is 1.6 percentage points above Additive, and their 95\% confidence
intervals do not overlap.

Classifier accuracy cannot establish temporal traceability by itself: it tests
whether the predefined context can be predicted, not whether changes in the
word's neighbors follow changes in its use across periods.

The contrastive test does not clearly separate the time-aware models. Additive reaches
\(0.602\) \([\pm0.053]\), and Token-Time reaches \(0.624\)
\([\pm0.048]\). Memory-Augmented reaches \(0.587\)
\([\pm0.051]\). Their confidence intervals overlap. Standard scores
\(0.000\) because it receives no period input.

In the continuation diagnostic, Additive and Token-Time again reach similar
accuracy (\(0.746\) vs.\ \(0.748\)), while Standard reaches \(0.717\) and
Memory-Augmented \(0.739\) (Table~\ref{tab:main-results}). Thus, Additive and
Token-Time behave similarly in D2 and D4, but Token-Time performs better when
the surrounding words provide no reliable context clue.

\subsection{Memory Ablations}
% Memory-Augmented: not a stable improvement over Token-Time; oracle diagnostic
% localizes the limitation to mean-vector aggregation.
The Memory-Augmented extension does not improve over Token-Time. Across 31
seeds, its continuation accuracy is \(0.739\) \([\pm0.008]\). Token-Time
reaches \(0.748\) \([\pm0.005]\). The paired difference is \(-0.009\)
\([\pm0.010]\).

Token-Time \([\pm0.009]\). Its sign-flip rate is \(0.694\)
\([\pm0.118]\), \(0.070\) above Token-Time. This estimate has a wide
confidence interval.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
% Target length: 1.25--1.5 pages.
The central result is twofold: temporal conditioning more than doubles neighbor
movement for drifting subjects, yet a learned token--time interaction produces
the same average movement as direct addition, and memory adds no reliable gain.
Stable subjects also move toward \(N_2\), showing that the temporal signal is
not fully selective.

Additive and Token-Time are equivalent when verbs and objects provide reliable
context clues (TOST, \(\delta=0.05\), \(p_\mathrm{TOST}<0.001\)). When those
words provide no reliable clue, the classifier gives Token-Time a small
advantage over Additive (\(0.595\) vs.\ \(0.579\)). The layer-wise CKA
comparison tells a complementary story: Additive--Token-Time similarity rises
from 0.726 at the input to 0.887 after the encoder. Their overall
representation patterns become more similar with depth, while the ambiguous
classifier still detects a condition-specific difference.

If ambiguity systematically favored Token-Time, its advantage should grow as
contextual cues become less reliable. The 15-seed noise sweep does not show
this pattern. For D2, the slope is \(+0.093\) (95\% CI
\([-0.279,+0.464]\), \(d_z=0.14\), \(p=0.601\)); for classifier accuracy, it
is \(-0.019\) (95\% CI \([-0.105,+0.067]\), \(d_z=-0.12\), \(p=0.644\)).
The ambiguous-condition result is therefore evidence for a specific condition,
not general Token-Time superiority.

Memory-Augmented does not improve over Token-Time. The oracle ablation shows
that the existing attention and gate can use summaries built from the known
\(N_1/N_2\) labels. However, replacing one average with two unsupervised
\(k\)-means cluster centers does not improve performance. These ablations rule
out a simple fix but do not isolate the remaining cause.

These results position the suite as a controlled architectural audit before
evaluation on natural LSCD data. RuShiftEval, for example, follows the same
words across three periods and includes change followed by stability as well
as continuous change~\cite{kutuzov2021rushifteval}. With annotated targets,
stable words can serve as negative controls, while gradual change and the gain
or coexistence of senses provide approximate counterparts to our drifting and
bifurcating trajectories.

A natural-corpus study can test whether the D2 equivalence persists when local
contexts identify the current sense and whether Token-Time gains an advantage
for more ambiguous usages. Such a study must adapt the diagnostics to
imperfectly annotated trajectories and account for open-ended polysemy,
metaphor, register variation, frequency shifts, and genre differences, none of
which the controlled SVO corpus reproduces.

In this controlled setting, direct addition is sufficient to produce the same
average neighbor movement as a learned token--time projection, while memory
provides no reliable gain. More complex temporal conditioning therefore
requires evidence from conditions in which local context is genuinely
ambiguous. More broadly, period or context-label recovery is not enough:
temporal traceability requires explicit word-at-period representations whose
neighborhoods can be inspected across time and whose changes reflect changes in
how the word is used.
